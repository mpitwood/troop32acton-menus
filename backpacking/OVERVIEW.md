# Backpacking Menu Repository

Same system as the Front-Country Overview — one page per course, browse
a la carte, vegetarian scouts get a real option, not a bolt-on — adapted
for trips where every ounce is carried on someone's back for days, and
every wrapper comes back out too. See the [Recipe List](#recipe-list).

## Where this started

This folder's structure, staple-food ideas, and calorie/protein targets
are informed by the 2026 Philmont Scout Ranch trail food menu (a document
dropped in `import/` for reference). Two things worth knowing about that
source before treating it as gospel:

1. **It's a resupply model, not a fully self-carried one.** Philmont crews
   pick up fresh bags every few days at backcountry commissaries, so their
   menu doesn't have to solve for 5+ days of food in one pack. **This repo
   is built for 3-night trips carried in full from the trailhead** — short
   enough that resupply isn't needed. If a future trek runs long enough to
   have a resupply point partway through, that changes the math (only half
   the food needs to be carried at once) — worth revisiting these
   quantities for that case rather than assuming they still apply as-is.
2. **Its vegetarian handling is an afterthought.** Nearly every breakfast
   and lunch bag defaults to a meat stick or jerky with no built-in
   alternative — the fix offered is "here's a link to buy your own
   substitute." Every recipe in this folder builds the vegetarian option
   in from the start, same as the front-country policy.

What's genuinely useful from that source and carried forward here: pairing
scouts into two-person "buddy" meal bags to halve packaging, leaning on
shelf-stable wedge cheese and meat sticks for lunch protein that needs no
cooking, freeze-dried/just-add-water entrées for dinner, and — most
importantly — **their actual calorie and protein numbers**, below.

## Weight, calorie, and protein targets

- **Daily calorie and protein targets are pulled directly from Philmont's
  own published nutrition facts**, not guessed. Summing one full day of
  their breakfast + lunch + dinner (the day-ending-in-1 rotation, using
  the nutrition labels printed for each component) comes to about
  **2,500 kcal and 90–95g of protein** — before the fresh fruit and
  shelf-stable milk Philmont hands out separately at commissary pickup,
  which adds more on top. They've fed thousands of teenagers through
  high-mileage, high-elevation multi-day treks for decades, so their
  numbers are a better baseline than a guess.
- Target **2,600–2,900 kcal/day from breakfast + lunch + dinner** in this
  repo — at or slightly above Philmont's baseline, since this repo
  doesn't have their separate fresh-fruit commissary stop to fill the gap.
- Target **90–100g of protein/day** across the three meals, matching
  Philmont's figure. Every page here states an estimated calorie *and*
  protein figure so you can add up a day's menu before buying anything —
  this is the one place in the whole repo with numeric nutrition figures
  on the page itself, because for backpacking, hitting a protein and
  calorie floor is a real planning constraint, not just a nice-to-know.
- **Snacks are the flexible add-on, not part of the core target.** A
  harder mileage or elevation day should pull more from the snacks
  folder; an easier day can skip them. Don't assume every day stacks a
  full breakfast + lunch + dinner + both snack pages — that oversuits the
  2,600–2,900 target by a wide margin. See the
  [sample day's menu](#sample-days-menu-per-person-3-night-trip-no-resupply)
  below for how this plays out with real page picks.
- Aim for **under 2 lb (32 oz) of food per person per day**, all three
  meals plus any snacks. Every recipe page states its weight per serving
  so you can add this up too.
- Target **at least 100 kcal per ounce** on dinner and snack items — the
  standard backpacking benchmark for keeping pack weight reasonable.
- Water is not counted in any of this — see your own water purification
  plan — but remember rehydration still needs *fuel* to boil, which is
  budgeted separately below.

## Cook methods, ranked by fuel cost

Every recipe is tagged with one of these, cheapest first. Default to the
cheapest method that still makes a meal someone wants to eat — don't pick
a simmer-heavy dinner every night just because it's tastiest on paper.

- **No-Cook** — eaten straight from the bag, zero fuel. Most lunches and
  grab-and-go breakfasts.
- **Cold-Soak** — rehydrates in cold water over 15–30 minutes (or
  overnight in a bag), zero fuel. Couscous, muesli, some noodles.
- **Boil-and-Steep** — bring water to a boil once, pour over food, cover
  and let sit 5–10 minutes off the heat. This is what most dinners here
  use. One boil, not a simmer.
- **Simmer** — needs sustained heat for several minutes (real rice, some
  lentils). Used sparingly, and noted clearly so you know which nights
  need more fuel.

**Fuel budgeting rule of thumb:** a standard 3.7 oz / 100g canister holds
roughly 6–8 person-days of boil-and-steep-only cooking (one boil per
dinner, occasional hot breakfast). Count how many Boil-and-Steep or
Simmer dinners are on the menu, add hot breakfasts, and size canisters
against that rather than guessing — carrying a whole spare canister "just
in case" is often heavier than the fuel it would have saved.

## Vegetarian policy (adapted from front-country)

- **Dinners** (the one-pot cooked meals) use the same **vegetarian base +
  meat boost** pattern as front-country: a shared pot that's vegetarian by
  default, with freeze-dried meat or chopped jerky added to individual
  bowls at serving time, never stirred into the shared pot.
- **Breakfasts and lunches** are mostly discrete items eaten from a bag
  rather than one-pot dishes, so the swap happens at the ingredient level
  instead: the meat stick or jerky is simply not the only protein in the
  bag — a nut-free plant protein (sunflower seed butter, roasted edamame,
  a plant-based jerky) sits right next to it as an equally real choice,
  not a special-order substitution.
- Where a boost is meat, it's sized "per meat-eating scout," same as
  front-country — don't buy or carry a full second dinner's worth of meat
  for one or two people.

## Allergy tags

Same definitions as the Front-Country Overview: **Nut-free** means no
tree nuts or peanuts and no unlabeled "may contain traces" products;
**Egg-free** means no egg ingredient. Backpacking leans hard on sunflower
seed butter as the peanut butter stand-in, same as front-country, since
it packs and rehydrates the same way.

## Packaging, bulk, and trash

Everything gets carried out, so the goal is to generate as little trash
as possible in the first place, and make what's left small and sealed.

- **Repackage everything at home before the trip.** Strip cardboard
  boxes, cracker sleeves' outer boxes, and any packaging bulkier than a
  zip-top bag. This cuts weight and bulk *and* trash at the same time —
  the box was never coming home either way.
- **Decant into the smallest bag that makes sense.** Personal ration bags
  for anything portioned per person (a day's trail mix, a dinner's worth
  of pasta); shared bags for things a cook group eats from together
  (spices, oil, a block of cheese cut down before the trip).
- **Don't burn wrappers.** Most snack and meal packaging is foil- or
  plastic-lined and won't fully combust — it leaves melted plastic in the
  fire ring, which is worse than packing it out. Pack it out, always.
- **Use a dedicated odor-proof trash bag** (a heavy-duty zip bag, doubled
  if needed, or a purpose-made odor-proof sack) kept separate from
  gear you touch often. Food scraps and used wrappers both go in it —
  smell doesn't care which one it came from.
- **Store the trash bag with the food bag or canister at night**, not in
  a tent or pack pocket — it smells like food to wildlife because it
  still has food residue on it.
- **Compress as you go.** Squeeze the air out of a wrapper before it goes
  in the trash bag; a flattened wrapper takes a fraction of the space of
  an inflated one over several days.
- **Portion accurately in the first place.** The biggest trash-and-weight
  win is not generating a food-waste problem at all — buy and pack to the
  calorie target per person per day, not "extra just in case," which
  usually comes home half-eaten and always comes home as pack weight.

## Pricing

Boston-area estimates, same sourcing basis as the Front-Country
Overview's pricing section — but several backpacking staples here (TVP, cheese powder, freeze-dried meat,
dehydrated vegetable flakes, coconut milk powder) are specialty items
usually bought from an outdoor retailer or online bulk-foods seller
rather than a grocery store, and Boston-specific pricing data for those
is thinner. Treat those cost estimates as rougher than the front-country
numbers, and check current prices before finalizing a shopping list.

Both paths land well under the 2 lb (32 oz)/person/day weight target,
and the core-meals-only total already sits close to Philmont's own
~2,500 kcal / ~90-95g protein baseline — add a snack on harder days to

combination is illustrative; swapping any main for another on the same
course keeps totals in a similar band since all pages in a course were
recalibrated to the same target.

Longer treks with a resupply option (5+ nights) would need different
portioning per leg — not covered by this folder yet.

note the extra Weight & Calories line and Packaging & Trash Notes
